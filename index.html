What is Money Laundering?

Money laundering is the process of making criminal proceeds appear legitimate — disguising where the money came from.

Common predicate offences

  • Fraud & financial crime
  • Drug trafficking
  • Corruption & bribery
  • Tax offences
  • Cybercrime
  • Terrorist financing

Counter-Terrorist Financing (CTF)

  • CTF controls are related but distinct
  • Terrorist financing can involve legitimate funds used for unlawful purposes
  • Small amounts can still pose serious risk
  • Same reporting obligations apply

The Three Stages of Money Laundering

Stage 1
Placement
Introducing illicit funds into the financial system — e.g. cash deposits, fictitious invoicing, currency exchange
→
Stage 2
Layering
Moving funds through multiple transactions to obscure their origin — e.g. wire transfers, shell companies, cryptocurrency
→
Stage 3
Integration
Reintroducing funds into the economy appearing legitimate — e.g. property, loans, payroll, investments
As a bookkeeper you may encounter any of these stages — particularly placement through client accounts and integration through apparently normal business transactions.

D

AML Requirements in Ireland

The primary legislation is the Criminal Justice (Money Laundering and Terrorist Financing) Act 2010, as amended. Bookkeepers are classified as Designated Persons and must comply.
Core obligations

Core obligations for designated persons

  • Risk-based approach — assess and document risk for each client
  • Customer identification — verify identity before providing services
  • Ongoing monitoring — keep client information current; review transactions
  • Record keeping — retain records for a minimum of 5 years
  • Report suspicious activity — file SARs with the Garda FIU
  • Staff training — all staff must receive regular AML/CTF training
Failure to comply may result in criminal prosecution, significant fines, and serious reputational damage to you and the firm.

Customer Due Diligence (CDD)

CDD means knowing your customer. You must verify who you are dealing with before providing bookkeeping services.
Obtain from every client
  • Full legal name
  • Residential or business address
  • Date of birth (individuals)
  • Government-issued photo ID
  • Company registration details (businesses)
Understand the relationship
  • Who the customer really is
  • Nature and purpose of the relationship
  • Expected volume and type of activity
  • Source of funds where relevant
  • Ultimate beneficial owner (UBO)
CDD is not a one-off exercise. You must update records when information changes and conduct ongoing monitoring of client activity.

Enhanced Due Diligence (EDD)

EDD is required for higher-risk customers. Standard CDD is not sufficient in these cases.
When EDD applies

Higher-risk scenarios

  • Politically Exposed Persons (PEPs) — current or former senior public officials and their close associates
  • High-risk jurisdictions — countries identified by FATF or the EU as high risk
  • Complex ownership structures — multiple layers of companies or trusts
  • High-value or unusual transactions not consistent with the client profile
  • Non-face-to-face relationships where identity cannot be verified in person
Additional EDD measures

What to do

  • Obtain evidence of source of funds
  • Obtain evidence of source of wealth
  • Seek senior management approval before onboarding
  • Conduct more frequent ongoing monitoring

Beneficial Ownership

The beneficial owner is the individual who ultimately owns or controls the customer — the real person behind the business.
Watch for
  • Shell companies with no clear business purpose
  • Complex chains of ownership across jurisdictions
  • Nominees acting on behalf of hidden controllers
  • Trusts used to obscure true ownership
Your obligation
  • Identify any person with 25% or more ownership
  • Verify their identity as you would a direct client
  • Check the RBO (Register of Beneficial Owners) in Ireland
  • Escalate if ownership cannot be established clearly

AML Red Flags

You do not need to prove wrongdoing — only to recognise that something appears unusual. When in doubt, escalate.
Client behaviour
  • Reluctance to provide ID or explain fund sources
  • Urgency without reasonable explanation
  • Transactions inconsistent with client profile
  • Third-party payments to/from unrelated parties
  • Requests to exclude normal controls
In the accounts
  • Revenue inconsistent with industry or location
  • Large unexplained payments or cash movements
  • Transactions involving high-risk countries
  • Structuring — multiple deposits just below €10,000
  • Suppliers with no online presence or address

Reporting Suspicious Activity

Reporting is a legal duty, not optional. You are protected when you report in good faith.
Internal — Step 1

Tell your MLRO immediately

  • Do not investigate yourself
  • Bring concerns to your Money Laundering Reporting Officer (MLRO)
  • Document what you observed, when, and in what context
  • Maintain strict confidentiality — do not discuss with colleagues unnecessarily

Suspicious Activity Report (SAR)

  • The MLRO decides whether to file a SAR with the Garda Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU)
  • The Revenue Commissioners are also a competent authority for tax-related laundering
  • Reports can be made online via the Garda portal
  • Do not delay — once there are reasonable grounds, file promptly

Tipping Off

Tipping off is a criminal offence carrying up to 5 years imprisonment. It occurs when you alert a subject that they are under suspicion or investigation.
Never tell the customer
  • That they are being investigated or monitored
  • That a SAR has been made or may be made
  • That authorities may be involved
  • That unusual activity has been flagged
Also avoid
  • Altering, destroying, or concealing documents once suspicion has arisen
  • Suddenly closing a client account without MLRO guidance — this can alert them
  • Discussing the matter with colleagues beyond those who need to know

Employee Responsibilities

AML & CTF compliance is everyone's responsibility — not just management.
Your obligations

Every team member must:

  • Follow all AML & CTF procedures in place at ABS Synergy Compliance Training
  • Complete AML training and keep knowledge up to date
  • Verify customer information and apply CDD correctly
  • Report concerns promptly to the MLRO — do not sit on suspicions
  • Protect confidential information related to any investigation
  • Never facilitate a transaction you know or suspect to be linked to crime
The Protected Disclosures Act 2014 protects employees who report concerns in good faith. You cannot be dismissed or penalised for reporting.

What is Financial Fraud?

Financial fraud occurs when deception is used to obtain money, assets, information, or financial advantage — whether by employees, customers, suppliers, or external criminals.
Who commits fraud
  • Employees and insiders
  • Customers
  • Suppliers and third parties
  • Organised criminal groups
  • Cyber criminals
Impact of fraud
  • Direct financial losses
  • Regulatory penalties and fines
  • Serious reputational damage
  • Loss of customer trust
  • Operational disruption

Types of Financial Fraud

Fraud can come from inside or outside the organisation — and increasingly from cyber-enabled attacks combining both.

Committed by employees or insiders

  • False or inflated expense claims
  • Payroll fraud — ghost employees, falsified hours, unauthorised bonuses
  • Theft of funds or physical assets
  • Procurement fraud — kickbacks, fictitious suppliers
  • Manipulation of accounting records
Example: An employee submits personal expenses — a weekend hotel stay — as a business cost, altering the receipt to show a client meeting.

Committed by individuals outside the organisation

  • Identity fraud — impersonating a legitimate customer or supplier
  • Account takeover — gaining unauthorised access to accounts
  • Payment diversion fraud — redirecting payments to criminal accounts
  • Loan application fraud — using false information to obtain finance
  • Fake invoices from fictitious suppliers

Technology used to commit or facilitate fraud

  • Phishing — fraudulent emails to steal credentials or money
  • Smishing — SMS-based scams
  • Vishing — phone call scams impersonating banks or suppliers
  • Malware attacks — software designed to steal data or disrupt systems
  • Business Email Compromise (BEC) — impersonating executives or suppliers to authorise payments

Fraud Red Flags

You do not need proof to raise a concern. If something feels wrong, it probably warrants a closer look. Escalate — do not investigate alone.
Employee
  • Unexplained wealth or lifestyle inconsistent with salary
  • Refusal to take annual leave
  • Excessive control over a process, refusing to delegate
  • Frequent policy breaches or overrides
  • Defensiveness when questioned about work
Customer
  • Unusual or inconsistent transaction activity
  • False or altered documentation
  • Inconsistent information provided
  • Requests to bypass normal procedures
  • Unexplained urgency
Supplier
  • Duplicate invoices for the same service
  • Requests to change bank account details
  • Lack of supporting documentation
  • Pressure for urgent or unusual payments
  • No verifiable address or online presence

Social Engineering & Cyber Fraud

Social engineering is the manipulation of people into revealing confidential information or taking actions that benefit a fraudster — exploiting trust, authority, or urgency rather than technical vulnerabilities.
How it works

Fraudulent emails designed to steal information or money

  • Urgency — "Your account will be closed in 24 hours"
  • Threats — "Failure to respond will result in legal action"
  • Unexpected attachments or links to fake websites
  • Poor grammar, unusual sender addresses, spoofed domain names
  • Requests for login credentials or payment details
BEC in detail

Fraudsters impersonate executives or suppliers

  • Fake urgent payment requests from a "CEO" or "director"
  • Emails requesting a change to a supplier's bank account details
  • Instructions to transfer funds quickly — bypassing normal approval
  • Often timed around month-end, holidays, or staff absences
Example: You receive an email appearing to be from the Managing Director asking you to urgently transfer €15,000 to a new account before end of day. The email address is almost identical to the real one — one letter different.
How to protect yourself

Always

  • Verify requests independently — call the person directly using a known number, not one provided in the email
  • Follow all approval procedures — do not bypass controls even under pressure
  • Use dual authorisation for payments above agreed thresholds
  • Never click links or open attachments from unexpected or suspicious emails

Preventing Fraud

Strong controls and consistent procedures are the most effective defence against fraud. Everyone has a role to play.
Segregation of duties

Split responsibilities

  • No individual should control an entire transaction process from start to finish
  • Separate who requests, approves, and processes payments
  • Rotate duties where possible to prevent over-reliance on one person
Verification procedures

Always verify

  • Customer identities before onboarding
  • Supplier information against known records
  • Bank account changes via a separate, verified contact
  • Invoices against purchase orders and delivery records
Approval processes

Follow authorisation limits

  • Adhere to payment approval thresholds
  • Escalate anything outside normal parameters
  • Use dual sign-off for high-value transactions
Record keeping

Maintain accurate records

  • Document all transactions with supporting evidence
  • Keep records secure and complete
  • Retain records in accordance with legal requirements

Reporting Fraud Concerns

If you suspect fraud, your first duty is to report it — not investigate it. Early reporting limits damage and protects the business and its clients.

✓ Do

  • Report concerns immediately through company procedures
  • Follow your internal escalation path — report to your manager or MLRO
  • Preserve all relevant evidence — emails, documents, records
  • Maintain strict confidentiality throughout
  • Cooperate fully with any investigation

✗ Do not

  • ✗Conduct your own investigation
  • ✗Alert or confront the suspected individual
  • ✗Delete, alter, or move any relevant records
  • ✗Discuss the matter unnecessarily with colleagues
  • ✗Ignore it and hope someone else reports it
You are protected under the Protected Disclosures Act 2014 when reporting concerns in good faith. You cannot be dismissed, penalised, or victimised for reporting.

What is GDPR?

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) is EU law that came into effect on 25 May 2018. It sets out how personal data must be collected, stored, used, and protected. In Ireland it is enforced by the Data Protection Commission (DPC).
  • Applies to all organisations that process personal data about people in the EU — including bookkeeping and accountancy firms
  • Personal data is any information that can identify a living individual — a name, address, PPSN, payroll number, email address, or bank account details
  • As a bookkeeper you handle personal data every day — client records, employee payroll, tax filings, supplier contacts, and financial statements
  • Non-compliance penalties can reach €20 million or 4% of global annual turnover — whichever is higher
  • Data Protection Act 2018 implements GDPR in Irish law and gives the DPC its enforcement powers

The 7 principles of GDPR

These seven principles underpin everything in GDPR. Every time you handle personal data, your actions should be measurable against them.
1
Lawfulness, fairness & transparency
Process data legally, fairly, and openly. People should know how their data is used.
2
Purpose limitation
Collect data for a specific, stated purpose. Don't use it for something different later.
3
Data minimisation
Collect only what you actually need. Don't hold more data than is necessary for the task.
4
Accuracy
Keep personal data accurate and up to date. Correct or delete inaccurate data promptly.
5
Storage limitation
Don't keep personal data longer than necessary. Apply and follow your retention policy.
6
Integrity & confidentiality
Keep data secure against unauthorised access, loss, or destruction. Use appropriate technical measures.
7
Accountability
The organisation must be able to demonstrate compliance — not just claim it.

Personal data & special-category data

Not all personal data carries the same risk. Special-category data requires extra protection and a higher legal threshold to process.
Personal data in bookkeeping
  • Client names, addresses, email addresses
  • Personal Public Service Numbers (PPSNs)
  • Employee payroll data and bank details
  • Tax returns and financial statements
  • Supplier contact information
  • Company director personal details
Special-category data

Requires explicit consent or another specific legal basis:

  • Health data — e.g. sick pay records, disability information
  • Trade union membership — relevant to payroll deductions
  • Racial or ethnic origin
  • Religious or philosophical beliefs
  • Criminal convictions data
Payroll example: An employee's sick leave record contains health information — special-category data. It must be stored separately with restricted access, not in a general shared folder.

Lawful basis for processing

You must have a lawful basis before you process any personal data. There are six bases under GDPR — identify and document the correct one before you start.
1
Consent — the individual has given clear, specific, freely given consent. They can withdraw it at any time. Rarely the right basis for employment or client data.
2
Contract — processing is necessary to perform a contract with the individual, or to take steps at their request before entering into one. Most common basis for client bookkeeping work.
3
Legal obligation — processing is required to comply with Irish or EU law. Covers Revenue filings, payroll tax, and statutory record-keeping.
4
Vital interests — necessary to protect someone's life. Rarely applicable in bookkeeping.
5
Public task — processing necessary for a task in the public interest. Applies mainly to public bodies.
6
Legitimate interests — your organisation has a genuine interest that is not overridden by the individual's rights. Can cover internal business records and fraud prevention, but requires a Legitimate Interests Assessment (LIA).

Data retention requirements

GDPR requires you to keep data only for as long as it is needed. Irish law and Revenue rules set minimum retention periods for financial and employment records — you must comply with both.
Record typeMinimum retentionLegal basis
Revenue / tax records6 yearsTaxes Consolidation Act 1997
Payroll records6 yearsRevenue / Employment law
Employee contracts & HR records7 years after employment endsEmployment law
VAT records6 yearsValue-Added Tax Act
AML records5 yearsCriminal Justice (ML) Act 2010
Client engagement records6 years after relationship endsLimitation Act / best practice
Accounting records6 yearsCompanies Act 2014
After the retention period ends, data must be securely deleted or destroyed. Simply archiving it indefinitely is a GDPR breach. A documented deletion schedule is required.

Secure handling of financial & payroll data

The integrity and confidentiality principle requires you to protect personal data from unauthorised access, accidental loss, or destruction. This applies to both digital and paper records.
Digital records
  • Use strong, unique passwords for all financial systems (QuickBooks, Xero, CollSoft, etc.)
  • Enable multi-factor authentication (MFA) on all cloud platforms
  • Share files via secure, access-controlled platforms — never via unencrypted email attachments
  • Lock screens when leaving your desk — even briefly
  • Use encrypted cloud storage (OneDrive, Google Workspace) — never personal accounts
Paper records
  • Store payslips, tax documents, and financial statements in locked filing cabinets
  • Never leave client files on desks when not in use
  • Shred sensitive documents — never put them in general waste bins
  • Do not remove physical files from the office without approval and logging
Common mistake: Emailing a payslip to the wrong employee. This is a data breach. Always double-check email addresses before sending payroll documents — use BCC for batch sends, or use a payroll portal instead.

Email, cloud storage & document-sharing risks

The most common GDPR breaches in bookkeeping firms involve accidental disclosure — sending data to the wrong person, using insecure platforms, or poor access controls on shared folders.
  • Wrong recipient emails — autocomplete can send a client's financial data to the wrong person. Always verify the address before sending sensitive data.
  • Over-shared cloud folders — a folder shared with "anyone with the link" containing payroll files is a serious breach. Use permission-based access only.
  • Personal devices and accounts — processing client data on a personal Gmail or Dropbox account falls outside your firm's security controls. Use work accounts only.
  • CC instead of BCC — sending a group email with multiple clients in the CC field discloses all their email addresses to each other. Always use BCC or individual sends.
  • Printer and fax risks — financial documents left on a shared printer or sent to the wrong fax number constitute a breach. Collect print jobs immediately.

Data controller vs data processor

Understanding whether you are a controller or a processor determines your GDPR responsibilities in each client relationship.
Data controller

Decides why and how personal data is processed. Bears primary GDPR accountability.

  • Your firm as an employer — you control employee and staff data
  • Your firm for its own internal records and marketing
  • Clients — they control their employees' payroll data
Data processor

Processes data on behalf of a controller, under their instructions.

  • Your firm when processing payroll data on behalf of a client — you are their processor
  • Software providers (Xero, QuickBooks, CollSoft) act as processors for your firm
  • Must have a written Data Processing Agreement (DPA) with each controller
If you process payroll or bookkeeping data on behalf of clients, you should have a signed Data Processing Agreement (DPA) in place with each client. This is a legal requirement under GDPR Article 28.

Accountability, DPO & Privacy Notices

The accountability principle requires your organisation to be able to demonstrate GDPR compliance — not just claim it. Several practical obligations flow from this.
Records of Processing Activities (ROPA)
  • Most organisations must maintain a written Record of Processing Activities
  • Documents what personal data you hold, why, how long, and who has access
  • Must be available to the DPC on request — failure is a violation
  • For bookkeeping firms: covers client data, payroll records, employee HR files, and supplier contacts
Data Protection Officer (DPO)
  • A DPO is mandatory for organisations that process special-category data on a large scale, or carry out large-scale systematic monitoring
  • Most small bookkeeping firms do not require a mandatory DPO — but appointing one voluntarily is good practice
  • The DPO must be independent, have expert knowledge of data protection law, and report to the highest level of management
  • Contact details of any DPO must be published and notified to the DPC
Privacy notices — transparency in practice
  • When you collect personal data, you must tell individuals what data you collect, why, the legal basis, how long you keep it, and their rights
  • This is usually done through a Privacy Notice (also called a Privacy Policy or Fair Processing Notice)
  • For bookkeeping firms: required for client onboarding, employee contracts, and any marketing communications
  • Must be written in plain language — not legal jargon — and provided at the time of data collection
Transfers outside the EU/EEA
  • Personal data may only be transferred outside the EU/EEA where adequate protection is guaranteed
  • Permitted if: the destination country has an adequacy decision from the EU Commission; or Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs) are in place; or the individual has given explicit consent
  • Relevant for bookkeepers using cloud software hosted outside the EU (e.g. US-based platforms) — check your vendor's data processing agreements

Data breaches & reporting

A data breach is any incident that leads to the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, alteration, unauthorised disclosure, or access to personal data.
  • Sending financial data to the wrong person — wrong email, wrong fax, wrong postal address
  • Lost or stolen device — a laptop, USB stick, or phone containing client data
  • Ransomware or cyber attack — unauthorised access to your systems or cloud accounts
  • Improper disposal — financial documents placed in general waste instead of being shredded
The 72-hour rule
  • If a breach is likely to pose a risk to individuals, you must notify the Data Protection Commission (DPC) within 72 hours of becoming aware
  • If the breach poses a high risk to individuals, you must also notify the affected individuals directly
  • All breaches — even minor ones — must be recorded in your internal breach register
  • Failure to report a notifiable breach is itself a GDPR violation and can attract fines

Individual rights under GDPR

GDPR grants individuals eight rights over their personal data. In a bookkeeping context, the most likely requests come from employees, clients, and former staff.
  • Right of access (Subject Access Request — SAR) — an individual can request a copy of all personal data you hold about them. You have one month to respond.
  • Right to rectification — correct inaccurate data promptly when requested
  • Right to erasure ('right to be forgotten') — delete data when there is no longer a lawful basis to hold it. Note: legal retention obligations may override this.
  • Right to restrict processing — the individual can ask you to pause processing while a dispute is resolved
  • Right to data portability — provide data in a structured, machine-readable format on request
  • Right to object — individuals can object to processing based on legitimate interests or for direct marketing

Subject Access Requests (SARs)

A Subject Access Request (SAR) is when an individual asks to see the personal data you hold about them. In bookkeeping, these often come from current or former employees, or clients requesting their own financial records.
How to handle a SAR
  • Step 1 — Verify the identity of the person making the request
  • Step 2 — Log the request with the date received — your one-month clock starts immediately
  • Step 3 — Search all systems for data relating to that individual (emails, payroll, accounts software, paper files)
  • Step 4 — Redact any third-party personal data before responding
  • Step 5 — Provide the response in a clear, readable format — free of charge
  • Step 6 — If you need more time (up to a further two months for complex requests), notify the individual within the first month
Failing to respond to a SAR within one month is a GDPR violation and can lead to a DPC complaint. Never ignore or delay a SAR without documenting the reason.

Client confidentiality & data protection

Client confidentiality is both a GDPR obligation and a professional duty. Financial data you hold about clients is highly sensitive and must never be shared without lawful authority.
  • Access control — only staff who need access to a client's data should have it. Apply role-based access in your accounting software.
  • No casual disclosure — never discuss client financial details in public places, on social media, or with colleagues who don't need to know
  • Third-party requests — if someone other than the client requests their financial data, you need the client's explicit written authorisation before disclosing anything
  • Statutory disclosures — Revenue, the Garda, or a court order may compel disclosure. Always take legal advice and document any such request before responding
  • Offboarding — when a client relationship ends, data must be returned, deleted, or retained only as required by law. Do not retain indefinitely.
